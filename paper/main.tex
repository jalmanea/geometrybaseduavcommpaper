\begin{abstract}
Unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) base stations are a promising solution for restoring connectivity in disaster scenarios where terrestrial infrastructure is destroyed, overloaded, or absent. However, a single UAV has limited capacity, and large numbers of ground users may simultaneously require connectivity, necessitating multiple drones operating as a swarm. Deploying such a swarm requires jointly determining how many drones to deploy and where to place them, balancing coverage against inter-drone interference. Existing approaches either rely on computationally expensive metaheuristic optimizers or ignore the directional, geometry-dependent interference created by antenna radiation patterns. We propose two user-aware heuristics that share K-means horizontal placement, nadir-pointing userlink antennas and minimum-spanning-tree backhaul pointing, and differ only in how they set altitude: an \emph{analytic heuristic} that derives a per-drone altitude from a closed-form beam-footprint rule, and an \emph{altitude-sweep} heuristic that selects one common altitude by direct search over $26$ candidate heights. Over 1{,}500 synthetic configurations, the analytic heuristic and the altitude sweep improve mean coverage over a fixed-altitude K-means baseline by $+26.3$ and $+30.2$ percentage points respectively, at mean deployment times of $5$ and $135$\,ms. On 24 real-world Telecom Italia snapshots of central Milan, the sweep beats the baseline on every snapshot at every fleet size, while the analytic heuristic leads it only with fewer than $8$ drones, exposing the limit of the closed form on weakly clustered demand.


% Evaluation over 1{,}500 synthetic configurations and 24 real-world Telecom Italia snapshots shows that the analytic heuristic achieves a $+26.3$ percentage point mean coverage gain over a K-means baseline across the synthetic grid at negligible computational cost. We then ask how much of that gain is attributable to the closed-form rule itself, by adding an \emph{altitude-sweep} heuristic that keeps the same placement and orientations but selects a single common altitude by direct search over $26$ candidate heights. The sweep adds a further $+3.9$ points overall, reaching $+30.2$ points over the baseline, wins on every distribution and at every fleet size $N \geq 4$, and removes the one synthetic regime in which the closed-form rule is beaten by the fixed-altitude baseline, at a mean deployment-time cost of $135$\,ms.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

Unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) base stations are a promising solution for providing connectivity in scenarios where terrestrial infrastructure is unavailable, overloaded, or impractical, such as disaster relief, temporary mass events, or coverage extension in remote and underserved areas~\cite{qiu2019,elhammouti2019,hydher2020}.
When multiple drones operate in close proximity sharing spectrum resources, inter-drone interference becomes a dominant performance-limiting factor~\cite{khuwaja2019}. The deployment problem, deciding \emph{how many} drones to deploy, \emph{where} to place them, and \emph{how} to orient their antennas, is a tradeoff: more drones increase spatial coverage but also increase mutual interference.

Existing approaches include heuristic placement methods such as K-means clustering~\cite{consul2024}, distributed methods for joint multi-UAV 3D placement and user association~\cite{elhammouti2019}, CNN-based rapid deployment methods for multi-UAV networks~\cite{wang2024}, and optimization-driven methods for multi-UAV deployment with jointly considered user access and backhaul connectivity~\cite{wang2025multiuav}. However, these methods typically do not tie each drone's altitude to the geometry of its own user cluster through the directional antenna pattern.
% Existing approaches include heuristic placement methods such as K-means clustering~\cite{consul2024}, optimization-driven methods for multi-UAV deployment with jointly considered user access and backhaul connectivity~\cite{wang2025multiuav}, and electromagnetic-compatibility-aware UAV formation adjustment methods~\cite{huang2025}. However, these methods typically do not exploit directional antenna geometry to derive closed-form altitude and orientation rules.

This paper asks how much of a multi-UAV deployment can be derived in closed form from antenna geometry, and what that costs relative to searching. We propose an \emph{analytic geometry-based heuristic} that derives positions, altitudes and antenna orientations from closed-form geometric relationships in milliseconds, with no objective-function evaluations, and measure it against an \emph{altitude sweep} that changes only the altitude assignment and selects it by direct search.

The key observation motivating our approach is that ground users rarely distribute uniformly; they naturally form spatial clusters around shelters, gathering points, or areas of interest. When a drone is placed above a cluster, the spatial extent of that cluster directly determines how high the drone should fly: a dense cluster requires a lower altitude to focus the beam, while a spread-out cluster requires a higher altitude for broader coverage. Furthermore, since each drone is placed directly above its cluster centroid, nadir-pointing naturally maximises the fraction of cluster users inside the main lobe, with no tilt required. Together, these observations reduce the deployment problem to closed-form geometric computations; the altitude sweep then measures how much coverage that closed form leaves on the table. Fig.~\ref{fig:system} illustrates the deployment setting.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We present a geometry-dependent interference model that jointly accounts for directional antenna patterns and probabilistic air-to-ground channels in a multi-UAV deployment setting.
    \item We propose an analytic geometry-based heuristic that derives complete UAV swarm deployments from closed-form geometric relationships with no iterative optimization.
    % \item We validate the nadir-pointing UL orientation through a per-drone tilt--azimuth sweep ($1^\circ$/$5^\circ$ resolution, 36 configurations), confirming that nadir-pointing leaves on average only $0.4$ percentage points of coverage to per-drone optimisation.
    \item We introduce an altitude-sweep heuristic that selects one common altitude by direct search over a coarse grid of candidate heights, and use it to quantify the coverage the closed-form rule gives up.
    % , and show that it dominates both the fixed-altitude baseline and the closed-form rule on every distribution and at every fleet size $N \geq 4$, at a cost of $26$ system-model evaluations per deployment.
    \item We evaluate 1{,}500 synthetic configurations (three user distributions) and 24 real-world Telecom Italia snapshots, and identify where each method wins.
\end{itemize}

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:system}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

We consider a square area of side $L = 2000$\,m in which $M$ ground users require connectivity from a fleet of $N$ UAV base stations. Each UAV~$j$ is characterized by its 3D position $\posv{j} = (x_j, y_j, z_j)$ and the orientations of two antennas: a userlink (UL) antenna serving ground users with tilt $\tau_j^{\mathrm{UL}}$ and azimuth $\alpha_j^{\mathrm{UL}}$, and a backhaul (BH) antenna for inter-drone communication with tilt $\tau_j^{\mathrm{bh}}$ and azimuth $\alpha_j^{\mathrm{bh}}$.

\subsection{User-Aware Deployment}

We assume that user positions are known prior to deployment. This models scenarios where user locations are obtained from cellular network historical records, crowd-sensing, or aerial imagery before the drone fleet is launched. Each deployment method receives the user layout as input and produces a complete configuration. %The same user layout is used for both deployment planning and performance evaluation.

\subsection{Antenna Radiation Model}

We adopt a parametric single-element pattern inspired by 3GPP~TR~38.901~\cite{3gpp38901}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:antenna_gain}
G(\theta) = G_{\max} - \min\!\left(12\left(\frac{\theta}{\theta_{3\mathrm{dB}}}\right)^{\!2},\; A_{\mathrm{SLA}}\right) \quad [\dBi]
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is the off-boresight angle, $G_{\max}$ is the peak gain, $\theta_{3\mathrm{dB}}$ is the full $-3\dB$ beamwidth, and $A_{\mathrm{SLA}}$ is the sidelobe attenuation.
The pattern is azimuthally symmetric around the boresight axis.

The off-boresight angle from drone~$j$ toward target position~$\vect{q}$ is:
\begin{equation}
\theta_{j} = \arccos\!\left(\bore{j} \cdot \hat{\vect{d}}_{j}\right)
\end{equation}
where $\bore{j}$ is the boresight unit vector determined by the tilt and azimuth:
\begin{equation}
\bore{j} = \begin{pmatrix} \sin\tau_j\cos\alpha_j \\ \sin\tau_j\sin\alpha_j \\ -\cos\tau_j \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
with $\tau_j = 0$ corresponding to nadir.
We use a UL antenna with $(G_{\max} = 18\dBi,\; \theta_{3\mathrm{dB}} = 60^\circ,\; A_{\mathrm{SLA}} = 20\dB)$ and a BH antenna with $(G_{\max} = 12\dBi,\; \theta_{3\mathrm{dB}} = 30^\circ,\; A_{\mathrm{SLA}} = 25\dB)$.

\subsection{Channel Model}

For air-to-ground links, we use the probabilistic LoS model of Al-Hourani et~al.~\cite{alhourani2014}:
\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{LoS}}(\phi) = \frac{1}{1 + a\,\exp\!\big(-b\,(\phi_{\mathrm{deg}} - a)\big)}
\end{equation}
with urban parameters $a = 9.61$, $b = 0.16$.
The average path loss combines free-space path loss with probabilistically weighted excess losses:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PL}(d, \phi) = 20\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4\pi d f_c}{c}\right) + P_{\mathrm{LoS}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{LoS}} + (1 - P_{\mathrm{LoS}})\,\eta_{\mathrm{NLoS}}
\end{equation}
with $\eta_{\mathrm{LoS}} = 1\dB$, $\eta_{\mathrm{NLoS}} = 20\dB$, and $f_c = 2$\,GHz.
Air-to-air backhaul links use pure free-space path loss.

\subsection{SINR and Interference}

Each user~$u$ is associated with the nearest drone $j^*(u)$ in 3D distance.
The userlink SINR is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sinr}
\mathrm{SINR}_u =\frac{P_{j^*,u}^{\mathrm{rx}}}{\sigma^2 + \sum_{j \neq j^*} P_{j,u}^{\mathrm{rx}}}
\end{equation}
where $P_{j,u}^{\mathrm{rx}} = P_{\mathrm{tx}}^{\mathrm{UL}} \cdot G^{\mathrm{UL}}(\theta_{ju}) \cdot 10^{-\mathrm{PL}/10}$ and $\sigma^2$ is the thermal noise power ($N_0 = -174\dBm$/Hz, $B = 10$\,MHz).
All drones share a single userlink frequency band (worst-case interference).

The total inter-drone backhaul interference accounts for directional gain at both ends:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bh_interf}
I_{\mathrm{total}}^{\mathrm{bh}} = \sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{\substack{i=1\\i>j}}^{N} P_{\mathrm{tx}}^{\mathrm{bh}} \cdot G^{\mathrm{bh}}(\theta_{ji}^{\mathrm{tx}}) \cdot G^{\mathrm{bh}}(\theta_{ij}^{\mathrm{rx}}) \cdot L_{\mathrm{a2a}}(d_{ji})
\end{equation}
Backhaul interference does not enter the userlink SINR~\eqref{eq:sinr}, and hence not coverage; the altitude sweep uses it only as a tie-breaker (Section~\ref{sec:sweep}).

\subsection{Performance Metrics}

A user is \emph{covered} if $\mathrm{SINR}_u \geq \gamma_r = 3\dB$, a lenient minimum-connectivity requirement below the range swept by Khuwaja et al.~\cite{khuwaja2019}, whose analysis shows that coverage area is sensitive to the choice of $\gamma_r$.
Since the noise power is fixed and all drones share one band, the only deployment-dependent term in the denominator of~\eqref{eq:sinr} is the co-channel interference. Coverage therefore measures how well a deployment keeps that interference below the serving signal, and coverage loss as $N$ grows is interference-driven; we report the mean user SINR alongside coverage where it exposes this mechanism (Fig.~\ref{fig:hotspot_vs_N}b).

We focus on coverage rather than throughput, since in emergency deployments the primary objective is to establish minimum connectivity for as many users as possible.

\section{Deployment Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

We compare three deployment methods, all user-aware, that share the same horizontal placement (K-means centroids) and the same MST backhaul orientation, and differ only in how they set drone altitude.
The K-means baseline uses a fixed altitude with nadir-pointing antennas; the analytic heuristic derives a per-drone altitude from the antenna geometry; and the altitude sweep selects a single common altitude by direct search.
Because the three share everything but the altitude assignment, the comparison isolates that one design variable.

\subsection{K-means Baseline}

The baseline places drones at the K-means cluster centroids of the known user positions.
All drones fly at a fixed altitude of 150\,m with nadir-pointing UL antennas ($\tau^{\mathrm{UL}} = 0$).
BH antennas are oriented via MST (Section~\ref{sec:analytic}, Step~4).
This method represents a standard user-aware placement with no geometric awareness of the antenna patterns.

\subsection{Analytic Geometry-Based Heuristic}
\label{sec:analytic}

The proposed analytic heuristic derives a complete deployment through four closed-form steps, summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:analytic}.

\subsubsection{Step 1: Horizontal Placement via K-means}
Drone $(x, y)$ positions are set at the K-means centroids of the user positions ($K{=}N$), identical to the baseline, placing each drone above the center of mass of its assigned cluster [Line~1].

\subsubsection{Step 2: Altitude from Beam-Footprint Matching}
\label{sec:altitude}

Altitude is a key design variable in UAV-assisted networks~\cite{zhou2019}, and with directional antennas its coverage-optimal value depends on the beamwidth~\cite{galkin2017}. Mozaffari et al.~\cite{mozaffari2016} use the footprint relation between altitude and beamwidth to place multiple UAVs at a common altitude over a circular target area, and He et al.~\cite{he2018beamwidth} jointly optimize the altitude and beamwidth of a single UAV hovering over user clusters. We instead set each drone's altitude from its own cluster's spread, under co-channel interference from the rest of the fleet, so that the UL antenna's $-3$\,dB footprint matches that spread.

For a nadir-pointing antenna with half-beamwidth $\theta_{3\mathrm{dB}}/2$, the ground-footprint radius is $z \tan(\theta_{3\mathrm{dB}}/2).$
We set this radius equal to the cluster spread $\sigma_j$, the pooled standard deviation of the users' $x$ and $y$ offsets from the centroid of cluster $j$ ($50$\,m for a single-user and $100$\,m for an empty cluster). This gives the altitude rule
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:altitude}
z_j = \frac{\sigma_j}{\tan(\theta_{3\mathrm{dB}}/2)},
\end{equation}
which is then clamped to the feasible range $[50, 300]$\,m.

Dense clusters are thus served from lower altitudes with tighter footprints, and dispersed clusters from higher ones.

\subsubsection{Step 3: Userlink Orientation}
Each drone points its UL boresight at the centroid of its assigned users [Line~6]. Since K-means places the drone above that centroid, this is nadir up to K-means' finite convergence, with a tilt under $1^\circ$ for most drones:
\begin{equation}
\tau_j^{\mathrm{UL}} \approx 0 \quad (\text{nadir})
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Step 4: Backhaul Orientation via MST}
A minimum spanning tree (MST) over the 3D drone positions is rooted at the \emph{gateway drone}, the drone closest to the area origin, where the ground infrastructure is assumed [Lines~7--11]. Each other drone points its BH antenna at its MST parent; the gateway points horizontally ($\alpha_g^{\mathrm{bh}} = 0$). Since backhaul interference does not enter the userlink SINR, this step does not affect coverage; it applies the same MST construction in all three methods.

\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\ENSURE Drone positions, UL and BH orientations

    \STATE $\sigma_j \leftarrow \mathrm{std}(C_j)$
    \STATE $z_j \leftarrow \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\sigma_j / \tan(\theta_{3\mathrm{dB}}/2),\; 50,\; 300\right)$
    \STATE $\tau_j^{\mathrm{UL}}, \alpha_j^{\mathrm{UL}} \leftarrow$ point toward centroid of $C_j$ \COMMENT{$\approx$ nadir}
\ENDFOR

\STATE $\tau_g^{\mathrm{bh}} \leftarrow \pi/2$;\; $\alpha_g^{\mathrm{bh}} \leftarrow 0$ \COMMENT{gateway points horizontally}
\FOR{$j = 1$ to $N$, $j \neq g$}
    \STATE $\tau_j^{\mathrm{bh}}, \alpha_j^{\mathrm{bh}} \leftarrow$ point toward $\posv{\pi(j)}$
\ENDFOR

The entire procedure involves one K-means run, $N$ closed-form altitude and orientation computations, and one MST construction, with a total complexity of $O(MN + N^2)$.

\subsection{Global Altitude Sweep}
\label{sec:sweep}

The analytic rule~\eqref{eq:altitude} infers altitude from cluster geometry alone: it never measures what that altitude achieves. The altitude sweep replaces the inference with a direct measurement. Horizontal placement, userlink orientation and backhaul orientation are exactly those of Section~\ref{sec:analytic}; only Step~2 changes.

Let $\mathcal{Z} = \{50, 60, \ldots, 300\}$\,m be a grid of $|\mathcal{Z}| = 26$ candidate altitudes spanning the same feasible range the analytic rule is clamped to. For each $z \in \mathcal{Z}$ the sweep places all $N$ drones at that common altitude, evaluates the resulting deployment with the system model of Section~\ref{sec:system} on the \emph{known} user layout, and retains the altitude with the highest coverage, breaking ties by $5$th-percentile SINR, then by lower backhaul interference~\eqref{eq:bh_interf}, and then by the lower altitude:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sweep}
z^{\mathrm{sw}} = \arg\max_{z \in \mathcal{Z}} \; C\big(\text{K-means}(N),\, z\big),
\end{equation}
where $C(\cdot)$ is the coverage of the fully specified deployment. Searching altitude by evaluation rather than deriving it is the approach taken by Hydher et al.~\cite{hydher2020}, who select UAV heights by constrained exhaustive search or particle swarm optimization; the sweep is the simplest instance of it, over one scalar.

Two properties matter for the comparison. First, the sweep is \emph{less} expressive than the analytic rule: it assigns one altitude to the whole fleet, whereas~\eqref{eq:altitude} adapts per drone. Any advantage it shows therefore comes from objective feedback, not from finer adaptation. Second, it costs $|\mathcal{Z}|$ evaluations of the system model instead of none, raising complexity to $O\big(|\mathcal{Z}|(MN + N^2)\big)$.

\subsection{Synthetic Configuration Sweep}
\label{sec:results_synthetic}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection{Experimental Setup}

We evaluate all methods over $50 \times 10 \times 3 = 1{,}500$ configurations: fleet sizes $N \in \{1, 2, \ldots, 50\}$, user counts $M \in \{100, 200, \ldots, 1000\}$ (a target for clustered and hotspot layouts, whose cluster sizes are random) and three user distributions.
Each configuration is evaluated over 20 independent Monte Carlo seeds. Each seed generates a fresh user layout from the specified distribution; the deployment method receives this layout, deploys, and is evaluated on the same layout. All reported metrics are averaged across seeds.

Three spatial distributions model distinct operational scenarios:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clustered:} 5 Gaussian clusters with spread $\sigma = 100$\,m, modeling evacuees gathered at shelters or triage points.
    \item \textbf{Hotspot:} 3 dense Gaussian clusters with spread $\sigma = 50$\,m, representing concentrated demand at a few locations (e.g., stadiums, transit hubs).
    \item \textbf{Uniform:} Users drawn uniformly at random over the $2000 \times 2000$\,m area, representing a baseline with no spatial structure.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Results}

\paragraph{Aggregate Performance}
Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage_by_dist} presents aggregate coverage across all 1{,}500 synthetic
configurations, broken down by user distribution.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{figures/fig_sweep_coverage_by_dist.pdf}
\caption{Mean coverage by user distribution over 1{,}500 synthetic configurations.
The altitude sweep leads on all three, and is the only method that does not lose to another on any of them.}
\label{fig:coverage_by_dist}
\end{figure}

On clustered and hotspot distributions the analytic heuristic gains $+37.6$ and $+42.5$ percentage points respectively over K-means.
On the uniform distribution, where users have no spatial structure, K-means with its fixed 150\,m altitude slightly outperforms the analytic heuristic ($89.5\%$ vs.\ $88.5\%$), because the altitude-adaptation formula infers a large cluster spread and, for $N \lesssim 30$, flies above $150$\,m, widening beams. However, even on uniform distributions, the analytic heuristic overtakes K-means at $N \geq 41$ as interference accumulates ($+3.5$\,pp at $N{=}50$).
Overall, the analytic heuristic leads by $+26.3$\,pp across all 1{,}500 configurations.

The altitude sweep leads by $+30.2$\,pp over K-means and by $+3.9$\,pp over the analytic heuristic, reaching $80.6\%$ mean coverage against $76.7\%$ and $50.4\%$. Its margin over the analytic rule is largest where that rule is least well matched to the layout: $+6.1$\,pp on clustered and $+4.5$\,pp on uniform demand, against only $+1.0$\,pp on hotspot demand, where both methods approach the $50$\,m altitude floor and the sweep has little left to find. The uniform case is the informative one: it is the single regime in which the closed-form rule loses to the fixed-altitude baseline, and the sweep removes that loss, reaching $92.9\%$ against $89.5\%$ for K-means and $88.5\%$ for the analytic rule.

Build times remain compatible with rapid deployment. K-means and the analytic heuristic average $2.3$ and $5.0$\,ms respectively; the sweep averages $135$\,ms and never exceeds $0.91$\,s across all $30{,}000$ runs, the cost of its $26$ system-model evaluations.

The two altitude-adaptive methods therefore trade model dependence against coverage. The analytic rule never evaluates the system model, so its altitude does not depend on the channel and antenna models being accurate, and it stays negligible when deployments are recomputed often or on board. The sweep's extra coverage is only as reliable as the model it searches, and its cost grows with $|\mathcal{Z}|$ and $MN$, exponentially so if altitudes were searched per drone. It suits a deployment planned offline with a trusted model, where that cost stays well under a second.

It is not a constant: the chosen height falls essentially monotonically with fleet size on both clustered and uniform demand, from $112$\,m to $50$\,m and from $267$\,m to $89$\,m respectively, and sits within $3$\,m of the $50$\,m floor at every fleet size $N \geq 4$ on hotspot demand. This is the same qualitative behaviour the analytic rule prescribes, obtained without any geometric model, and it explains why the fixed $150$\,m baseline is mismatched at both ends of the fleet-size range.

\paragraph{Per-Configuration Breakdown}
Fig.~\ref{fig:hotspot_vs_N} reveals the key asymmetry between methods as fleet size grows.
On the hotspot distribution, K-means coverage collapses rapidly, from $64.8\%$ at $N{=}5$ to effectively $0\%$ by $N{=}40$, because its fixed
150\,m altitude spreads beams too wide once drones become dense relative to the hotspot footprint,
so neighbouring beams overlap and co-channel interference swamps the serving signal.
The analytic heuristic adapts altitude to the inferred cluster spread, keeping beams focused and
sustaining $92.0\%$ at $N{=}5$ and still $22.1\%$ at $N{=}50$; the sweep tracks it closely at $93.5\%$ and $23.3\%$, since on hotspot layouts both approach the $50$\,m altitude floor as $N$ grows.
Fig.~\ref{fig:hotspot_vs_N}b shows the interference mechanism behind this divergence: under K-means the mean user SINR falls below the $\gamma_r = 3$\,dB threshold at $N{=}11$, whereas both altitude-adaptive methods keep it above the threshold up to $N{=}33$ (analytic) and $N{=}34$ (sweep).
On clustered distributions the separation is much wider: the analytic heuristic plateaus at approximately $80$--$88\%$ for $N \geq 10$ and the sweep at $86$--$93\%$, while K-means degrades monotonically from $94.2\%$ at $N{=}5$ to $12.0\%$ at $N{=}50$. At $N{=}50$ the three methods stand at $12.0\%$, $80.2\%$ and $86.4\%$.

\paragraph{Scaling with Fleet Size}
The sweep is above both other methods at every fleet size $N \geq 4$ on all three distributions, and below $N{=}4$ it is never behind by more than $0.1$\,pp. On uniform demand it is the only method whose coverage \emph{increases} on net with fleet size, from $93.0\%$ at $N{=}5$ to $93.5\%$ at $N{=}50$, because it is free to descend as the cells shrink while the baseline is stuck at $150$\,m and the analytic rule over-estimates the spread of a structureless layout.

\subsection{UL Orientation Validation}
\label{sec:orientation_validation}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

To test nadir pointing, we optimise each drone's UL tilt ($0^\circ$--$60^\circ$, $1^\circ$ steps) and azimuth ($5^\circ$ steps) individually, holding all other drones at their defaults, over 36 configurations ($N \in \{5,10,\ldots,30\}$, $M \in \{200, 400\}$, three distributions, $5$ seeds). Re-orienting a single drone recovers on average $0.40$\,pp of coverage for the analytic heuristic (max $0.78$\,pp, uniform, $N{=}5$) and $0.34$\,pp for the sweep (max $0.71$\,pp, hotspot, $N{=}25$), against $0.77$\,pp for K-means (max $4.47$\,pp, hotspot, $N{=}5$), so no single drone gains much by tilting. Applying every drone's best angle at once recovers more, $+3.9$\,pp for the analytic heuristic and $+3.3$\,pp for the sweep on average ($+7.7$\,pp for K-means), so nadir pointing is a good default but not jointly optimal.

\subsection{Real-World Validation}
\label{sec:results_telecom}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection{Experimental Setup}

To validate the heuristics on realistic user distributions we use the Telecom Italia
Milan dataset~\cite{barlacchi2015,telecomitalia2015milan}, which records SMS, call, and internet activity
in 10-minute bins for each square of a $100 \times 100$ grid of $235 \times 235$\,m
squares covering the city. For each (date, hour) pair we:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Identify the $10 \times 10$-cell crop with the highest aggregate activity
          via a sliding-window maximum (hottest $2{,}350 \times 2{,}350$\,m patch).
    \item Sample 800 user positions from the crop, weighted by per-cell activity,
          and rescale to a $2{,}000 \times 2{,}000$\,m area.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Balanced snapshot design:}
We use a balanced $4 \times 2 \times 3$ design over 24 snapshots: four weeks (Nov~3/4, Nov~9/14, Nov~20/24 and Dec~5/8), one weekday and one weekend day per week, at 10:00, 15:00 and 20:00 UTC.

\textbf{Replication and seed design:}
We use $R = 20$ seeds per condition; each seed draws an independent K-means initialisation and a subsample of $M$ users from the 800-user pool (the full pool at $M = 800$), shared by all methods (paired design). The reported subset comprises $24 \times 26 \times 8 \times 3 \times 20 = 299{,}520$ evaluations ($N \in \{5,\ldots,30\}$, $M \in \{100,\ldots,800\}$, 3 methods). We report $M = 800$; the ordering is the same when pooled over $M$.

\subsubsection{Results}

Fig.~\ref{fig:telecom_coverage} shows coverage at $M = 800$. The sweep beats K-means on all 24 snapshots at every $N$; with seeds averaged within each snapshot, the paired $95\%$ confidence interval of the gain excludes zero at every $N$. The margin is largest at $N{=}5$ ($+6.3$\,pp) and smallest at $N{=}16$ ($+0.13$\,pp, CI $[0.11, 0.15]$). The analytic heuristic leads K-means only for $N \leq 7$ ($+5.8$\,pp at $N{=}5$) and trails it for every $N \geq 8$, by up to $6.3$\,pp at $N{=}16$ and $2.9$\,pp at $N{=}30$, both at $M = 800$ and pooled over $M$. Milan demand is broad rather than clustered, so, as on uniform synthetic demand, the footprint rule infers a large spread and flies high, from about $300$\,m at $N{=}5$ to $170$\,m at $N{=}30$, against about $250$ to $110$\,m for the sweep, which leads it by $5.4$\,pp on average. Means over $N$ are $90.7\%$, $89.1\%$ and $85.3\%$ for the sweep, K-means and the analytic heuristic.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------

We presented a closed-form geometry-aware heuristic for UAV base station deployment that derives altitudes and antenna orientations directly from user geometry and antenna beamwidths at negligible computational cost, and an altitude-sweep heuristic that instead selects one common altitude by direct search over $26$ candidate heights. Across 1{,}500 synthetic configurations and 24 real-world Telecom Italia snapshots the sweep never loses to a fixed-altitude K-means baseline. The closed-form rule wins by wide margins on clustered synthetic demand, but on weakly clustered layouts it overestimates the altitude: on measured Milan demand it leads the baseline only with fewer than $8$ drones.

On synthetic layouts the sweep improves on the closed-form rule by $+3.9$\,pp, and by $+5.4$\,pp on measured demand, at the cost of $26$ system-model evaluations per deployment. Both methods inherit K-means horizontal placement, the sweep assigns a single altitude fleet-wide and depends on an accurate system model, and the MST backhaul optimises link distance rather than interference. Future work will derive the altitude target from the coverage objective rather than from the $-3$\,dB footprint convention, integrate frequency reuse, and extend both methods to dynamic user distributions.


% Comparing them quantifies how much the closed form gives up. The sweep leads it by $+3.9$\,pp on synthetic layouts and $+7.2$\,pp on measured demand, and is the better method at every fleet size $N \geq 4$, despite being fleet-wide rather than per-drone. Altitude is therefore worth searching wherever a system model is available at deployment time, and the geometric rule is best understood as a zero-evaluation approximation to that search rather than a substitute for it.
